A book is years of someone's thinking, sometimes a whole life of it, compressed into ten or fifteen hours you can carry in one hand. **Nothing else gives you that much of another mind for so little.**

Reading goes at your pace, not the author's. You can stop, go back, read a hard paragraph three times, argue in the margin and pick it up again next week. **A book gives you the reasoning, not only the conclusion**, and the reasoning is the part you need in order to disagree well. A feed hands you the takeaway with the argument stripped out. A book has room for the objection, the counterexample and the place where the author isn't sure.

Some ideas only fit at that length. They need two hundred pages of groundwork before the point lands, and an idea that survived its author defending it for two hundred pages is a different thing from one that fit in a post. Books also reach people nothing else can. **Almost everyone worth learning from is already dead, and reading is how you get into the room with them.**

Following one argument for hours is a skill, and people who stop practising it say they lose it. A book is also one of the few things you still choose on purpose. Every feed you scroll was arranged by a system optimising for something other than your interests; you pick the book, and nobody gets paid when you keep going.

The research points the same way, with one honest limit: it compares people who read with people who don't, so it can't prove that reading caused the difference.

- Yale followed 3,635 adults over 50 for up to 12 years. **Book readers had a 20% lower risk of dying during the study**, and books did better than newspapers and magazines. After controlling for health, wealth and education the survival gap is about four months, not the two years often quoted.
- Rush University tested 294 older people every year until they died, then examined their brains. Those who read and wrote most declined about 32% more slowly, even with the same physical damage in the brain. Reverse causation is possible: early decline can make people read less.
- Across 31 countries, the number of books at home at age 16 predicted adult literacy and numeracy beyond the parents' education and the person's own schooling. Most of the gain comes from the first 80 or so books.
- In 54 studies with more than 170,000 readers, people understood factual text better on paper than on screen, and the gap grew over the years instead of closing. For novels there was no difference.
- A meta-analysis found fiction improves how well you read other people, by a small but real amount. The popular version, that a few pages of literary fiction make you more empathetic, did not replicate.

You will also see "six minutes of reading cuts stress by 68%". It traces to a 2009 press mention with no published study behind it, so it is left out here.

Books are not the only way to learn from people. **Listening to someone talk for hours gives you what a book can't: how they think, live.** That case is made in [Why listen to long conversations](/with-claude/why-listen-to-long-conversations/). The two work together.

**Read for what someone knows. Listen for how they think.**
